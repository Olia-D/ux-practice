# Writing about your own work

Everything below serves one rule: **what I did → what changed → who can confirm it.** One sentence per item. Three to five items, not ten.

People writing about their own work systematically undersell it. Assume the raw material is stronger than the draft describes, and that the job is tightening upward rather than trimming down.

## 1. Channel check

Is this an artefact or a conversation?

- **Artefact** — read once, fast, with no follow-up question possible: a review form, a written summary, an impact log entry, a CV. Lead with the conclusion and attach the evidence under it.
- **Conversation** — follow-ups are expected. Don't lead with conclusions; show the path that got you there.

Getting this backwards is the most common failure: showing your work in a form nobody reads twice, or presenting finished conclusions to someone who will argue with them.

## 2. Plane check

Garrett's five planes: surface, skeleton, structure, scope, strategy. Seniority is judged at scope and strategy, almost never at surface. Scan the draft — if the items are mostly naming, layout, component or visual decisions, it is undersold. Find the scope and strategy decisions underneath them and lead with those.

## 3. Evidence form

Prefer a measured before-and-after. When impact is upstream or shared — the usual case in design — do **not** invent a number to fit the formula. Use a counterfactual plus a named person who can confirm it:

- Weak: "Improved the onboarding experience."
- Strong: "Instrumented the flow; the data showed [finding], which is what the proposal rests on — the engineering lead saw the same data."

Never state a figure the user has not confirmed. Never describe their role as larger than it was; overstatement invites scrutiny and costs more than it gains.

## 4. AI framing

Three facts, in this order:

1. What AI did, with a sense of scale.
2. **What the user decided** with the time or output — which option to test, what to scope out, what to stop.
3. What that saved or protected.

Never lead with hours saved, versions generated, or tool-usage rankings. Leading with tool output makes the tool the hero and the person look smaller. Managers increasingly weight judgment about what to delegate; the judgment is the claim.

## 5. Scope, never workload

Describe breadth as areas owned against areas hired for. Avoid "overwhelmed", "too much", "stretched". Workload framing routes to a capacity answer; scope framing routes to levelling.

## 6. Cut these words

Scan every draft and remove: helped, helped with, supported, assisted, just, a bit, somewhat, tried to, was involved in, contributed to (when they led it), kind of, sort of, I think maybe. And the self-adjectives: passionate, dedicated, hardworking, team player, detail-oriented.

If a summariser would reduce a sentence to "generic", delete the sentence.

## 7. The repeatable line

Open with one sentence a manager could say out loud in a room the user is not in. Write it for them to quote, not for the user to feel accurate.

## 8. Forward asks, not confessions

Any "what would you like to develop" field is an ask about scope, expectations or support — not a list of shortcomings. Development answers are read in calibration alongside everything else.

End a review or a year-summary with the bigger problem the user wants to own next.

## 9. Verify before shipping

List, separately from the draft, every number, quote and characterisation that has not been confirmed, so the user can check them. Never bake unverified figures into text that enters a company system.

## 10. Calibrate to the evidence

One mention is "mentioned once", not "expert in". A proposal is a proposal until it ships and is measured — say so rather than implying a result.

## Output

The draft, then a short verify list. Suggest keeping a copy outside the employer's system — the same text gets reused in levelling and pay conversations later.
