# Design Critique Sessions — Pattern Reference

Critiques turn into opinion fights when nobody agreed what kind of meeting it was. Name the
meeting type first; each one has different rules about who speaks and what happens to the
feedback.

## Four meeting types

| Type | Purpose | Who | What happens to feedback |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Critique** | Improve the craft of work in progress | Designers only | Asked as questions, not prescriptions. The designer decides what to use. |
| **Design review** | Check the work against goals and constraints | Cross-functional | Each point is implemented or answered with a reason. |
| **Quality review** | Keep patterns and components consistent | Led by a design lead | Directive: fix it to match the system. |
| **Shareout** | Show progress and business value | Anyone | One-way. No feedback expected. |

## Setup

- **4–6 people.** More and the silent ones stop contributing.
- **Rotate roles:** captain (owns the calendar), facilitator (keeps time), presenter,
  critics, note-taker. Rotating stops the same voices dominating.
- **60–90 minutes, in 30-minute slots.** Presenters send their topic 24–48 hours ahead,
  with the stage of the work and the feedback they want.

## The 30-minute slot

| Minutes | Step |
|---|---|
| 2 | Background: the problem, the user, the constraint |
| 3 | Walkthrough of the work |
| 2 | Clarifying questions only — no opinions yet |
| 7–10 | **Silent written feedback** (sticky notes or comments) |
| 10–13 | Conversation |
| 3 | Wrap-up: the presenter says what they'll do next |

Silent feedback first is the step that matters most: it gets everyone's view before the
loudest person anchors the room.

**Discussion order:** people from other disciplines first, then designers, then leads.
Seniority speaking last keeps juniors from simply agreeing.

## Good critique questions

- What is the primary user benefit, and does this design deliver it?
- What other ways could deliver the same benefit?
- Which decision here are you least sure about?
- What happens when it goes wrong — empty, error, slow, no permission?

A polished artifact can hide missing decisions. If the team can't name the user benefit,
that is a valid finding in itself — match fidelity to how settled the concept is.

## Presenting work for feedback

State the goal, the stage, and the feedback you want up front. Write headings as takeaways.
Show rejected alternatives and the trade-offs. Tell the user's context, not a screen tour.
For executives: shorter, and framed as business outcomes.
