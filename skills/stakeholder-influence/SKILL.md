# Influencing a decision you don't control

The governing fact: **you cannot win opinion battles with people more senior than you.** The only real lever is information they do not have. Everything below follows from that.

Before drafting anything, answer these four:

1. What information do I have that they don't?
2. Is my ask a conclusion, or a path they can walk?
3. Who has veto here, and have I aligned with them first?
4. Am I re-arguing something? If so, what is actually new?

If question 1 has no answer, the conversation is not ready. Go get the information first.

## Veto or input

Not all stakeholders are equal. Some can block the work; others can only influence it. Spend energy accordingly — effort poured into an input-only relationship that feels urgent is usually anxiety, not strategy.

Align with the person who owns the area **before** going to their manager, so leadership gets one read rather than two competing ones.

## Show the path, don't sell the conclusion

Presenting a conclusion invites them to compare their preference against yours, and you lose that comparison by default — their preference carries more weight. Walk the line instead:

**outcome → what we found → what we tried → where it landed**

Four sentences in a corridor; expanded in a 1:1. The structure stays the same at every length.

Open by restating the shared goal and asking whether anything has changed. That sets the scope of the conversation before solutions enter it.

## Ask what you missed

After showing the problem space, ask whether they see anything missing. This converts the dynamic from presenting-to into contributing-with, and they often hold context you don't. Capture what they say even when you disagree; it can be tested later.

## Never counter a hunch with a hunch

If the natural reply is "I don't think we should build X", replace it with a question that surfaces the problem: *what are we actually solving — are we behind the market, differentiating, or responding to customer demand?*

This reads as senior rather than obstructive, and it moves the conversation out of the solution space where you cannot win.

## Don't shoot down their idea

Fastest way to lose trust. Instead:

- If it addresses a different problem: "That has promise — it fits when we get to [that problem]."
- If it rests on a faulty assumption: map it together and let them find the assumption themselves. Do not name it for them.

## Priorities, not ownership

With protective or territorial colleagues, ownership language triggers defensiveness. Priorities framing dissolves the same conflict without anyone losing face: *given the current priority is X, where does this fit?*

Avoid arguing about roles or who owns what. Make competing work redundant rather than wrong.

## Inform, don't ask permission

Short frequent updates beat a big reveal. By the time you present a batched update, they have formed their own conclusions in the gap and you are back in an opinion battle.

The goal is that a protective stakeholder is never surprised — that is the part you control. Whether they take offence is not. For anything touching their area: a brief heads-up, an open invitation, then proceed.

## When overruled

More senior people sometimes overrule you; that is their prerogative. Do not turn it into a debate about how decisions should be made — never "this is how it's supposed to be done". Do the work well under the constraint and let the result argue. Results persuade; ideology does not.

**Never re-argue a position someone took recently without new information.** Restating the same case in the same conversation hardens it. Wait for a new fact or a new window.

## Naming people

Name colleagues as verification of your own work — someone who saw the same data, confirmed the finding, ran the test. Not as public endorsement of theirs; help with their work privately.

## Afterwards

Send a short written summary of what was agreed, with names and dates: *as discussed, you'll look into X by Y.* Verbal agreement is not commitment, and process without a date stays vague until it is written down.
