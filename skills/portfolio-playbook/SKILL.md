# Portfolio playbook

The best portfolio is short, relevant and easy to judge: 2–3 outcome-led case studies a reviewer
can understand in 30 seconds and believe in 10 minutes.

Unifying principle:
> A portfolio is judged on what the reviewer can decide quickly, not on how much work it shows.
> Every element should help them answer: who is this, what do they solve, and is it worth opening?

Frame every finding as **what the reviewer does because of it** (closes the tab, skips the case,
doubts the number) — not as taste.

## Modes

- **REVIEW** — audit a portfolio, homepage, case study or interview deck against the rules below
- **WRITE** — draft or restructure a case study, card copy, hero line or one-pager
- **PLAN** — position for a target role and choose which projects to show
- **PRESENT** — build the interview deck, timing, appendix and STAR stories
- **TAILOR** — adapt case order, intro line and one-pager to a specific job ad

Start every mode by asking (or inferring): target role and level, domain of the target company,
and which case is most relevant to it.

---

## 1. Why portfolios get rejected

Reviewers often look at 20–30 portfolios a day and decide within about 30 seconds whether to keep
reading. A CV gets 6–10 seconds. In those 30 seconds they want: who is this (name, role, level,
location), what problems they solve (domain and kind of work), and a reason to open a case.

| Rejection reason | What the reviewer sees | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quality issues | Typos, blurry images, broken links, inconsistent type | Proofread, check links, one type scale |
| Not relevant | B2C visuals for a B2B role, no domain match | Lead with the case closest to the job |
| Process theatre | Linear template: research, personas, wireframes, done | Show decisions, trade-offs, what went wrong |
| No outcome | Screens without a result | A number or concrete change on every case |
| Unclear role | "We" everywhere | Say what you did, with "I" |
| Too long | 20-minute reads, every artifact | About 10 minutes; cut what doesn't support a decision |
| Hidden contact | No CV or email in sight | CV and email visible on every page |

Reviewers score roughly on craft, communication, collaboration, product thinking and impact.

## 2. Positioning comes first

Relevance beats being special: the reviewer asks "can this person solve *our* problems?"
1. One target role per version (separate CVs per track; change case order, not the site).
2. Name the domain in one line — domains, not a split of years per skill, so the reader doesn't box the person in.
3. One-line value proposition: what you do and for whom, in plain words. Test: could a recruiter repeat it?
4. Put the differentiator in the work, not in adjectives.
5. Mirror the job: pull 3 pains from the ad; the first case answers at least one.

Ban: "passionate", "delightful experiences", "user-centric", "crafted". Say what changed.

## 3. Choosing the case studies

2–3 strong cases, not 10 thin ones; ideally different problem types (e.g. one 0→1, one growth or
redesign of something live). Score candidates on: relevance to role/domain, ambiguity of the problem,
personal ownership, measurable or concrete result, real screens available (confidential data
removed), and tension (a pivot, conflict, failed first try).

- Lead with the most relevant case, not the newest or prettiest; reorder per application.
- Label work type accurately on each card (0→1, Redesign, Enablement). Adding a capability to an existing product is not 0→1.
- Show the role on every card.
- Unshipped work counts if its impact is shown (directions tested, roadmap shaped, risks found).
- Leave out concept redesigns of famous apps and anything that can't be explained end to end.

## 4. Writing a case study

A story about decisions, about 10 minutes, result first.

1. **Outcome title** — what changed, not the feature name ("Taking approvals out of email and Slack", not "Approval flows"). "How I [did X] to [achieve Y]" also works.
2. **Hook and hero** — one sentence plus the final design above the fold.
3. **Context** — company, product, users, team size, role, timeline, constraints, in 3–5 lines.
4. **Problem and why it mattered** — who was hurting, how you knew, what it cost.
5. **The messy middle** — 2–4 decision moments: considered, rejected, tested, changed course. Arc: "we realised X, so we did Y, which led to Z".
6. **Result** — numbers first with baseline and time frame, then qualitative change.
7. **Reflection** — what you'd do differently now.

Equivalent frames: Problem → Hypothesis → Test → Results → Learnings; Problem → Strategy →
Execution → Result; STAR. Use one shape on every case.

Rules: "I" for your work, "we" for the team's · show the messy details (a perfectly linear process is a
red flag) · explain every artifact or cut it · no template headings for their own sake, no tool
walkthroughs · subheadings that state findings · human voice · ~3–5 laptop scrolls, ~40% text / 60% visuals.

Results: "Improved the experience" → "[metric] down [X%] in [period] after launch". No hard numbers →
concrete change (process removed, decision made, direction killed early, a quote). **Never invent,
round up or illustrate with a number the person can't defend in an interview** — ask for the real
figure, its baseline and source.

## 5. Visuals and the quality bar

- Real screens first; before/after pairs; blur confidential data rather than dropping the screen.
- One key visual per section, caption states the benefit, not the screen name.
- Show device, task and state (empty, error, success), not only the happy path.
- Consistent art direction across all cards; mixed styles read as unfinished.
- Crisp over decorative: one radius, small palette, no box-in-box backgrounds.
- Legible at card size — crop tighter or enlarge.
- Multi-size WebP/AVIF, explicit width/height, alt text that says what the image shows.
- Before publishing: no typos (read aloud), no broken links, consistent type and spacing, checked on a phone.

## 6. The homepage

One job: get the right case opened. On a 13-inch laptop the first screen shows who, what and at
least one result without scrolling.

Order: hero (name once, role, city, two-line value proposition, one domain line) → 2–3 case cards,
the biggest elements on the page → 1–2 testimonials linking to their case → how you work, visible as
text → contact (one CTA, email as text + Copy button) → footer (CV, LinkedIn, email).

Card: about four elements — image · number first ("29% fewer tickets") · outcome title in 6–8 words ·
role + work-type pills. Small plain logo in a corner; a visible arrow or underline so it reads as
clickable without hover.

Hard-won lessons:
- Numbers above the fold, but never on top of the design — under the image.
- Two-line headline, not three; every extra line pushes cards down.
- Nothing important behind hover (touch and keyboard users never see it).
- No auto-moving content without a pause control (WCAG 2.2.2).
- Pick one way to separate things; tiles + outlines + shadows + pills adds noise fast.
- Contrast on everything clickable; white pills on white disappear.
- mailto alone isn't enough — show the address and add Copy.
- Mock 2–3 layouts side by side before building.

## 7. Site craft

| Area | Minimum bar |
| --- | --- |
| Accessibility | Contrast ≥ 4.5:1, visible focus, links not by colour alone, alt text, one H1, full keyboard access |
| Motion | Only on user action; honour reduced motion; nothing moving > 5 s without pause |
| Performance | First screen < 2 s on 4G; compressed images; no layout shift |
| Mobile | Checked at 375 px; tap targets ≥ 44 px; no horizontal scroll |
| Honesty | Every number traceable; dates and roles accurate |
| Contact | CV and email from every page; public CV without phone or home address |
| Details | Custom 404, favicon, page titles and meta descriptions that match content |

Personal touches (short warm About, photo, an opinion, a telling hobby) help memorability if they
don't get in the way.

## 8. One system: portfolio, CV, LinkedIn

Recruiters check all three in the same few minutes; one mismatch and they trust none.
- One source of truth for numbers (metric, baseline, period, source); copy from it, never memory.
- Same role title and value proposition everywhere for the track.
- Link both ways: portfolio in CV header and LinkedIn Featured; CV and LinkedIn on the portfolio.
- When a number changes, update all three the same day.

## 9. Presenting in interviews

Build a separate, shorter deck — don't scroll the website live.

| Part | Time |
| --- | --- |
| Intro: who, focus, why this role | 2–3 min |
| Case 1: most relevant, problem → outcome | 10–15 min |
| Case 2: shorter, different problem | ~10 min |
| Q&A and wrap-up | 5–10 min |

8–10 slides: title + value proposition, snapshot, case 1 problem + outcome, 3–4 process slides,
case 2, collaboration, principles, why here / why now, Q&A. Show the result early; decisions not
steps; one headline per slide; pause for questions; appendix for follow-ups; slides that stand alone
(scorecards are filled in afterwards); send a one-pager before and the link after; rehearse to time.

Prepare 6–8 STAR stories from the cases: conflict with PM/engineer, failure, influence without
authority, hard trade-off, feedback acted on, leading. First answer under 2 minutes, end with the learning.

## 10. Feedback and iteration

Cheap tests first, one change at a time:
1. 5-second test with a non-designer: who, what, what would you click?
2. Silent read of one case by a designer: can they state problem, role, result?
3. 2–3 expert reviews (e.g. ADPList mentors in the target domain).
4. Proofread aloud, on a phone, again the next day.

| Pattern | Likely problem | Change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Few callbacks | CV or first screen | Positioning line, CV metrics, case order |
| Calls, no interviews | Tailoring | Mirror the ad, lead with the matching case |
| Interviews, no offers | Storytelling | Deck, STAR stories, rehearsal |

Tailor without rebuilding: reorder cases, adjust the intro line, send a matching one-pager.
5–10 thoughtful applications a week beats mass applying.

## Checklists

**Before publishing:** first screen shows name, role, city, value proposition and a result at
~1280×650 · 2–3 cases, most relevant first · every card has image, number, outcome title, role, type ·
every case has context, problem, 2–4 decisions, result, reflection · "I" vs "we" correct · ~10-minute
reads · nothing hover-only · nothing auto-moving without pause · a11y checked · phone and slow
connection checked · no typos or broken links, favicon and 404 · CV and email on every page, Copy button.

**Before applying:** 3 pains from the ad · case order and intro match the role · numbers identical
across portfolio, CV, LinkedIn · CV current, PDF, no phone on public copies · one-pager ready ·
a warm route in (referral, recruiter, short note to the hiring manager).

**Before the interview:** separate 8–10 slide deck rehearsed to time · 60–90 s intro and 5–7 min
pitch practised aloud · appendix · 6–8 STAR stories · tailored "why here, why now" · 2–3 questions
for them · thank-you note ready to send within 24 hours.

## Output formats

**REVIEW**
```
❌ ISSUE: [label] — where it appears
What the reviewer does: [behaviour this causes]
Fix: [specific change, with rewritten copy where relevant]

✅ STRONG: [label] — why it works
```
End with the 3 highest-impact fixes, ranked.

**WRITE** — deliver the case study in the 7-part structure, flag every number that still needs a
baseline or source as an open question, never fill it with a guess.

**TAILOR** — the 3 pains from the ad → which case answers each → new case order → rewritten intro
line → one-pager draft.

**Related skills:** card, hero and CTA copy → `ux-writing`; layout, controls and accessibility of
the site → `interaction-design-reviewer` (`references/visual-design.md` for hierarchy and colour);
charts of results → `data-visualization`; turning research or metrics into a defensible result →
`ux-research-advisor`.
