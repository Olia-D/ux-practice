    Reframing Interaction Design Basics

    How to simplify 8 foundational UX laws so early-career designers actually understand them

    Summary Working with early-career UXers on website redesigns exposed consistent gaps in how foundational interaction design principles are understood. This training module reframes each law in plain language — cutting theory, keeping meaning.

    Most interaction design laws are taught the wrong way. The academic definitions are technically correct but practically useless when a designer is staring at a nav menu trying to decide what to do. Here are the 8 most misunderstood principles, reframed for real-world use.

    01
    Fitts's Law

    Put stuff as close to the user's cursor as possible. Make the most important things bigger.

    02
    Law of Magic Pixels

    Users look for things in corners and can reach them easily. Use corners for global elements.

    03
    Hick's Law

    Break things into categories and steps when they get complicated. Reduce decision load.

    04
    Progressive Disclosure

    Show users what they need when they need it. Just-in-time information.

    05
    Miller's Law

    Don't make users remember things or think too hard. This isn't about the magic number 7.

    06
    Affordances & Signifiers

    Affordance: make it look like what it does. Signifier: tell users what will happen.

    07
    F-Shaped Reading

    For large content blocks, design for the F-pattern scan — users read across the top, then down.

    08
    Jakob's Law

    Users spend more time on other websites than yours. Leverage familiarity. Don't reinvent the wheel.

    The Hierarchy Rule

    Fitts's and the Law of Magic Pixels seem to contradict each other. They don't — context resolves the tension. Use corners for global elements (navigation, logout, settings). Place contextual elements near the predicted cursor (action buttons near the thing being acted on). Jakob's Law is the overriding principle: when in doubt, default to what users already expect.

    Every Icon Needs a Label

    A data-driven recommendation backed by 11 studies over 19 years

    Summary Decades of UX research — including 11 first-hand studies across 5 companies — show that unlabeled icons confuse users and reduce accessibility. The "clean look" argument doesn't hold up against usability data.

    For decades, UX research has shown that unlabeled icons confuse users and make interfaces harder to use. This isn't theory. Across multiple projects over 19 years at 5 different companies, the results have been consistent: icons without labels break usability.

    The Problem

    In 2010, during a Minitab interface redesign requiring 1,000+ custom toolbar icons, usability tests revealed a familiar and damning pattern: users couldn't identify what icons did. Even power users — daily users of the software — failed to match icons to their functions.

    The Studies

    Study 1 — Icon-to-description matching: Two columns: icons in random order, short command descriptions also randomized. Users had to match them. Result: failure. Even power users spent too much time guessing.

    Study 2 — Icon-to-label matching: Replaced descriptions with command names (labels) to bias the test in favor of icon-only design. Users still couldn't reliably match icons to labels, even with the simplified test conditions.

    What the Evidence Says

    • Nielsen Norman Group research confirms labels improve usability and accessibility
    • Baymard Institute data supports labeled icons for navigation tasks
    • Unlabeled icons force users to guess or rely on tooltips — slowing task completion
    • Screen reader users are discriminated against entirely when icons have no text equivalent
    Despite this, some stakeholders and designers still push for icon-only buttons, claiming it reduces clutter in a way that equals better usability. Clean design doesn't always equate to a functional one. — Trevor Calabro

    How to fight the "cleaner look" argument

    Run a 20-minute icon matching study: print your icons, list your command names on a separate sheet, ask 5 users to match them. Share the results. The data makes the argument for you without requiring a debate about aesthetics.

    RememberAn icon without a label is a guessing game. Label it.

    Make It Look Like What It Does

    A real-world guide to affordances and signifiers — what "intuitive" actually means

    Key Definitions Affordance = The user can tell what to do just by looking at it.
    Signifier = The user knows what will happen if they do it.
    This is what "intuitive" actually means.

    These are Don Norman's foundational concepts from The Design of Everyday Things. Despite being decades old, many aesthetically driven UXers haven't heard the terms — or have confused them into meaninglessness.

    Affordances

    An affordance is what something looks like that tells people what it does. If it looks like a button and most people try to click it — that's a good affordance.

    If you show it to 100 people and 95 say "that looks like a button," your affordance is strong. If people stop and think about what something is before interacting with it, your affordance is weak.

    This logic applies consistently: tabs should look like tabs, toggles should look like toggles, input fields should look like areas where you can type. Remove all doubt.

    Signifiers

    A signifier is a cue that tells users what will happen after they interact. The distinction matters:

    • A button that looks clickable is an affordance
    • The label "Save" on that button is a signifier
    • A grey button saying "Save (unavailable until all fields are complete)" addresses both

    The Quick Test

    Show the element to a person who didn't design it. Ask two questions: 1) "What would you do with this?" (tests affordance) and 2) "What do you think will happen if you do that?" (tests signifier). If either question produces a hesitation or wrong answer, something needs to change.

    Most UI Animations Shouldn't Exist

    A case against anything auto-triggered

    Summary Most UI animations that play automatically make products harder to use, not easier. Auto-triggered motion harms usability, increases cognitive load, and creates accessibility barriers. The data is clear.

    This is not an anti-animation position. It's a position against animations that move without being triggered by an explicit user action.

    The Worst Offenders

    • Autoplaying videos (especially homepage heroes and help content)
    • Auto-pulsing or auto-prompting chatbots
    • Carousels that rotate automatically
    • Animated GIFs used as icons or decorative elements
    • Auto-blinking elements meant to draw attention to new features
    • Animated cursors or elements that follow the cursor
    • Interrupt pop-ups

    What the Data Shows

    A Bureau of Internet Accessibility study found that over 92% of users consider auto-animations "annoying." Nielsen Norman Group has documented that users frequently describe auto-animating content as "plain annoying," especially when it interferes with reading or navigation.

    In user testing, people rarely describe auto-animating elements as delightful. The word "annoying" shows up in the literature more than any other descriptor.

    The Illusion of Delight

    The common justification: animations make things feel smoother, more human, more delightful. But in practice, many auto-triggered animations add friction rather than remove it. They make users wait. They make users question what's happening. They cause users to miss important information.

    True user delight comes from a system that anticipates a user's needs and delivers a better solution than they could have imagined. Real delight is giving users full control and helping them do things right the first time. — Trevor Calabro

    The Rule

    If a user didn't trigger it, it probably shouldn't move. Every auto-triggered animation should be able to answer: what problem does this solve for the user at this exact moment? If the answer is "it looks cool" or "it feels modern," it's serving the designer, not the user.

    RememberIf the user didn't trigger it, it shouldn't move.

    The Problem With Split Buttons

    My "Famous" Split Buttons Rant

    Summary Split buttons keep failing in the real world because they hide options behind a tiny, low-discoverability target and break the common mental model of one button, one action. Decades of research have already proven this, yet many UXers still insist on using them.

    Split buttons almost always harm usability and accessibility. That's a statement that has gotten into many debates over the years, but it's not a hot take. It's a fact backed by decades of moderated studies, analytics, and well-documented HCI research.

    Three e-commerce clients recently asked for validation of their split buttons after being recommended to adopt a more standard pattern. All three pushed back. They pointed to competitors, made unfounded claims about how their users are special, and one even said "I don't think our users will have trouble with split buttons because they're on our site all the time." During a quick demo, that same person mis-clicked the dropdown arrow themselves.

    What Are Split Buttons

    Split buttons look like a single-button UI element, but they're divided into two parts offering two separate functions — a primary labeled action plus a small dropdown indicator icon (usually a down arrow) that reveals related options. The problem is simple: they look like standard select controls that users interact with far more often, creating a persistent affordance mismatch.

    Why Stakeholders Keep Requesting Them

    Stakeholders tend to like split buttons because they avoid hard decisions about priority. "We can put every possible action in one button? Cool, let's do that!" The split button feels like a compromise that saves space. Unfortunately, saving space is not the same as reducing cognitive load.

    The Two Most Common Types

    • Primary action controls: When designers can't decide which actions deserve priority, they merge everything into one control and hide alternate options under the arrow — instead of placing two clear buttons side by side.
    • Secondary option controls: In e-commerce, used when someone wants a single action like Add to Cart but also wants to expose Save for Later, Add to Wishlist, or Buy With Different Payment. The compromise costs real usability.

    What Users Actually Do

    In usability testing, users consistently treat split buttons as single buttons. They interpret the tiny dropdown indicator arrow as decorative. The hidden options go undiscovered. And when users intend to access the dropdown but accidentally trigger the primary action — as happens repeatedly in testing — they're confused and frustrated.

    The Anatomy of an Input Field (Parts 1 & 2)

    The 6 Elements — Quick Reference

    1. Required indicator (*): Left of label, contrasting color. Every required field needs one, even if all fields are required.
    2. Label: Explicit, succinct, persistently visible above the field. Never use placeholder text as a substitute for a label.
    3. Optional indicator: Use "(Optional)" on the right only when a mix of required and optional fields would otherwise be confusing.

    Stop Weaponizing Jakob's Law

    What the law actually says — and how teams misuse it to justify bad design decisions

    Summary Jakob's Law is one of the most useful principles in interaction design and one of the most misapplied. Teams routinely invoke it to justify novel or stripped-down interactions by finding a single screenshot somewhere on the internet. That's not what the law says. The law is about prevalence — patterns users encounter constantly across many tools they actually use.

    Jakob's Law: users spend most of their time on other sites, so they expect your site to work the way those sites work. Any convention used on the majority of products users interact with gets burned into their mental models, and deviating from it creates usability friction. In practical terms: don't reinvent the wheel.

    The misuse pattern: a team introduces a novel interaction. A usability concern is raised. Someone finds a single screenshot from a niche analytics tool or enterprise product doing something similar and invokes Jakob's Law. What was missing: any serious look at how often users encounter that pattern across the tools they actually use every day.

    One team introduced hidden controls in a dense data table to look "cleaner." When users couldn't find them in testing, a screenshot from one specialized tool was presented as Jakob's Law proof. But users don't spend most of their time in that tool. They spend it in products with visible, labeled controls — which is the actual population the law refers to.

    The 3-Question Test When Jakob's Law Gets Invoked

    1) What is the user actually trying to accomplish? 2) How often do users of this product encounter this pattern across the digital tools they use daily — not just one example you found? 3) Is there behavioral evidence that users successfully use this pattern elsewhere, or just a screenshot? All three need real answers before the law applies.

    Stop Weaponizing Miller's Law

    7 ± 2 is about memory, not menus

    Summary Miller studied how much people can hold in working memory once information is gone. A visible menu requires no memorising, so his finding says nothing about how many items it should have. Citing it to cap menus at seven ends conversations instead of testing whether the structure works.

    "Trim the menu to seven — Miller's Law" is one of the oldest misuses in UX. Miller's research asked people to repeat information they could no longer see. Interfaces that genuinely tax working memory exist — a verification code carried between screens, values compared across pages — but those are design flaws to remove, not a rule for lists.

    Two ways it gets weaponized

    • As a shortcut. Nine items become seven, and nobody asks whether the labels are clear or the grouping matches users' mental model.
    • As a conversation-ender. Once a famous study is invoked, questioning the decision sounds like questioning science — so nobody shows the original was confusing or the new one is better.
    Don't swap 7 for 4Newer research puts working-memory capacity nearer three or four items under lab conditions. That is not a new menu limit either. Familiarity, grouping and whether items stay visible matter far more than the count.

    Ask better questions instead

    Mega menus routinely show dozens of links successfully when they're grouped into user-centred categories with familiar labels. Three vague, overlapping options can fail where twenty clear ones succeed. Ask: do labels match users' words? Do categories match how they think? Are we hiding useful options just to hit a number? Can people complete their tasks? Tree testing, card sorting, search logs and usability tests answer those; a count doesn't.

    Line to use in the meeting

    Miller's research was about remembering things that are no longer visible. These options stay on screen — let's check whether users can understand and navigate this structure.

    RememberMiller measured memory for what's gone; a visible menu asks for no memorising, so 7±2 doesn't cap it.

    Primary Navigation Interaction Guide

    A free reference for interaction, accessibility and implementation

    Summary A spec-ready rule set for primary navigation: activation is click, tap, Enter or Space — never hover. Every item is either a link or a menu button, never both. Hover is visual only, focus is visible and distinct, and the semantics use native HTML before ARIA.

    Calabro published the navigation spec he'd refined over ten years of writing similar documents. It's written to be handed to engineering.

    Interaction

    • Two item types. Destination items are links with no indicator. Menu items are buttons with an indicator and an expanded/collapsed state.
    • No split rows. A row navigates or opens a menu, never both. If the parent has its own page, put an "Overview" link inside the menu.
    • Hover is decoration. It can't open, close or dismiss anything, change ARIA state or tab order. Moving the pointer away doesn't close an open menu.
    • Dismissal. Re-activate the trigger, click outside, press Esc (focus returns to the trigger), or open another menu.
    • Animation. 100–150 ms, never delaying access or hiding focus; respect reduced-motion.

    Keyboard and semantics

    • Focus is visible and distinct from hover, current and expanded states; focus alone never opens a menu.
    • Links respond to Enter; buttons to Enter and Space. Closed child items leave the tab order. No focus traps except a modal mobile panel.
    • Use a labelled <nav>, lists for items, aria-expanded kept in sync, one aria-current="page". Avoid role="menu" and aria-selected for site navigation.
    • Labels in users' language; icons need visible text; the mobile trigger says "Menu".

    Information architecture

    Keep hierarchy flat, expose destinations as end points, group children by user expectation into medium-sized groups, and avoid duplicate destinations. Mobile behaves like desktop: sections expand on activation, not hover.

    A framework for usable and accessible help systems

    01
    Just-in-time

    Information appears when the user makes the next decision, not before they need to remember it.

    02
    Only what's necessary

    Up-front steps only for what the system needs to work, or security and compliance.

    03
    Explicit next action

    Minimum text needed to make the next step obvious — not minimum text.

    04
    Relevant only

    Adapt to role, permissions, product access and workflow stage.

    The Rules of AI Chat History

    A real-world framework for designing good chatbot experiences

    Summary A chat transcript is a record, not a history. Calabro's model splits AI chat into three connected layers — task history, interaction history and an output workspace — so users can tell tasks apart, scan what happened, and find the current result without scrolling through old versions.

    Layer 1 — task history

    The most important decision is what counts as a separate task: a self-contained request with a goal and a finish point. Ask whether the user is still working toward the same outcome, whether the new instruction refines the existing output, and whether earlier context could distort the answer. A subject change isn't always a new task.

    • One thread per distinct goal; refinements stay inside it; users can rename threads in their own words.
    • People keep typing into whatever chat is open. When the system detects a real shift, suggest a new task in user terms — "so each request's progress stays separate" — never in terms of tokens. Let them decline.
    • Long-running work (multi-source research, analysis, reports) gets its own background instance, listed by state: needs clarification, in progress, ready for review, completed, failed.

    Layer 2 — interaction history

    Separate events from results: the original instruction and final output matter most; routine tool activity matters only when something fails. Mark milestones at real changes of direction, collapse older exchanges into compact items that describe their content ("Completed analysis with 4 flagged changes", not "Updated response"), and keep warnings and open questions visible.     Layer 3 — output workspace

    Anything long, editable, versioned, exported or reused lives in a persistent panel beside the conversation, with the current version obvious and earlier ones available for comparison.

    How to test it

    Give participants a pre-populated history and ask them to: return to a task from days ago, find the final output after several revisions, say which sources informed an answer, start an unrelated task without contaminating the current one, find the task waiting for review, and explain what context the system is using now.     Usability Tests vs. Concept Tests

    A usability test observes behaviour; a concept test collects opinions

    Another Way A/B Tests Mislead

    Causal inference, confounds, and the limits of correlation

    Summary Even well-designed A/B tests can mislead when confounds are present, when sample sizes aren't adequate for the effect size, or when novelty effects inflate results. Causal inference is harder than it looks.

    Causal inference is the science of figuring out whether one thing actually causes another — not just whether they're correlated. A/B tests are supposed to establish causation by controlling for confounds. But in the real world, confounds are everywhere, and many teams run underpowered tests that produce misleading signals.

    Common Confounds in A/B Tests

    • Novelty effects: Users behave differently with a new design simply because it's new. Effect decays over time.
    • Segment contamination: Users exposed to both variants (cross-device users, shared accounts) corrupt the control/treatment separation
    • Time effects: External events (seasonal traffic, marketing campaigns) affect conversion rates during test windows
    • Sample ratio mismatch: If traffic isn't split as planned, results are invalid

    The Booking.com Lesson

    Booking.com runs more A/B tests than almost any company in the world. Their data science team has documented that they're wrong about their hypotheses roughly 9 out of 10 times. If a company with that infrastructure and expertise is wrong at that rate, smaller teams with less rigorous test conditions are likely wrong more often.

    Mixed Methods Studies That Mix

    What real integration of qual and quant looks like in practice

    Summary Most teams say they're doing mixed methods when they're really just running two separate studies. Real mixed methods research integrates qual and quant from the study design stage — not from the reporting stage. If the synthesis only happens after all data is collected, it's already too late.

    Just because you used both qual and quant methods doesn't mean you did mixed methods research. People run a usability test and a survey in the same sprint, put the outputs in one report, and call it mixed. What they've done is parallel research, not integrated research.

    Here's the difference: Parallel studies are separate projects using different methods that might answer related questions but are designed and analyzed independently. Mixed methods studies are intentionally structured so that the methods inform each other — answering the same questions from different angles, integrated from the start, not just in the reporting phase.

    Real mixed methods work happens at the study design level. It requires making decisions about how methods connect, how timing affects interpretation, and how synthesis will work before you start collecting data. Without alignment in purpose, scope, and integration, all you've got is two isolated data sources and a hope that they don't contradict each other too much.

    The 4 Mixed Methods Designs That Work

    • Exploratory sequential (qual → quant): Start with interviews, diary studies, or field work to explore the problem space. Then design a survey or behavioral study to test, validate, or size what you found. Use when you're in early discovery and don't yet know the key variables.
    • Explanatory sequential (quant → qual): Identify a pattern in the data — a usage drop-off, a SUPR-Q dip, unusual success metrics — then do qual work to explain what's going on. Turns unexplained variance into actionable insight.
    • Concurrent: Collect both simultaneously and triangulate. Useful when you need both breadth and depth at the same time and have resources for both tracks.
    • Embedded: One method is nested inside another — e.g., a usability test that includes a brief survey at the end to quantify patterns observed qualitatively.

    A Real Example

    You interview patients about how they interpret blood pressure trend graphs. You find their mental models differ: some see a stable line as healthy, others assume the device isn't recording properly. Then you run a survey to measure how common each mental model is and how it affects trust in the system. That's exploratory sequential. The qual shaped what the quant measured. That's the integration that makes it mixed methods.

    The Most Common Error Running a usability test and a survey independently, then combining the findings in a deck at the end. The synthesis must be planned before data collection starts. The findings of one study should directly shape the questions or instruments of the other.

    Cognitive Biases in UX Research

    20 biases in three groups, each with an antidote

    Summary Calabro's guide began as a reference document for one UX team and spread to others.     Reframing Accessibility

    Accessibility is usability under real-world conditions — not a separate checklist step

    Summary Teams separate accessibility from usability — a discrete step, a compliance exercise, a checklist tacked on at the end. Trevor's argument: in practice, accessibility is a proxy for good usability. Every accessibility failure is a usability failure, usually affecting more users than just those with disabilities.

    While consulting with a large org, Trevor reviewed a website with a team that felt very good about how it looked. Clean UI, good use of white space, balanced typography. Then they walked through top user tasks the way a real user would. The vibe in the room shifted.

    Important help content was hidden behind hover interactions. Placeholder text disappeared when clicking into fields. Prime nav bar behaviors differed between desktop and mobile. Content users needed to compare was tucked inside tabs, forcing memory use instead of side-by-side comparison. None of these friction points seemed too bad in isolation — but experienced back-to-back in a workflow, they were obviously broken.

    The design looked clean because the interface was offloading effort onto the user. That's the pattern: polished UI often hides poor UX. Accessibility failures are where that offloading becomes most measurable and most costly.

    The Reframe

    Accessibility isn't a second pass. It's a first-principles usability standard. Help content hidden behind hover: fails users without hover capability and fails users who don't discover it. Placeholder labels that disappear on click: fails screen readers and fails sighted users who forget what they were entering. Color-only status indicators: fails color-blind users and fails users on degraded displays.

    Every accessibility requirement maps directly to a usability best practice. Treating them as separate workstreams means doing the same work twice — and often doing it wrong both times.

    Research Rigor at Product Speed

    Avoid becoming the bottleneck

    Summary Rigor isn't applying the same standard to every study. It's setting the minimum evidence a specific decision needs — before research starts — and then cutting operational waste rather than methodology. Three questions set the bar; a four-step threshold makes it explicit.

    Not all decisions are equal

    Checking whether users understand a navigation label needs a handful of sessions. Showing that a redesign improves completion needs a defined metric, a larger sample and a proper comparison. Both are rigorous when the method fits the conclusion — the error is claiming more than the method supports, like estimating prevalence from five sessions.

    01
    What if we're wrong?

    A flow that triggers accidental payments needs stronger evidence than a label.

    02
    Can we reverse it?

    Easily reversed choices may only need directional findings.

    03
    How much uncertainty is OK?

    All research has some. Understand it, say it, and decide if it's acceptable.

    A worked threshold

    For a new navigation, Calabro sets the goal of finding problems that affect at least 20% of users with 85% probability. The binomial approach (MeasuringU) gives about nine participants. Proving a completion-rate improvement is a different question with its own sample-size calculation.

    Cut waste, not rigor

    Recruiting, formatting reports, scoring and rebuilding materials eat time. Calabro's AI-assisted tool for PURE scoring and reporting was about 5.5× faster with the method unchanged — and became the organisation's standard. Templates, participant panels and reusable protocols do the same.

    Say what you learned, precisely. Not "users prefer this design" but what the sessions showed, what that suggests, and what's still unknown. Teams can proceed on current evidence with stated risks and a post-launch check — that's still rigorous if it meets the agreed threshold.

    Stop Throwing Away Time-on-Task Data

    The truth about small-sample task time

    Summary Small-sample timing data is skewed: everyone has a minimum time, but delays are open-ended. Lead with the median, show the range and individual times, explain the slow cases from what you observed — and never delete the outlier, because it's usually the failure path.

    Practitioners either dismiss eight timings as meaningless or trim the slowest one to make the report "clean". Calabro calls trimming risky and unethical: the slow case is often the most important finding.

    1. Use the median
      It represents typical observed behaviour when data is skewed.
    2. Show the range and individual times
      With eight people, the individual times are often the clearest chart.
    3. Explain the slow cases
      Link each one to what happened in the session and the design issue behind it.
    4. Qualify the claim
      Describe this study, not the user population.

    For formal comparisons use non-parametric tests, bootstrapped intervals or transformations, and be cautious with p-values on tiny samples.

    When the mean matters

    At analytics scale. A flow completed 500,000 times a month that adds 20 seconds per run is a real aggregate cost — abandonment, support load, errors. Report the mean there; keep it in the appendix for an 8-person study.

    A/B Test Strategies That Work

    Winning isn't the same as learning

    Summary An A/B test only compares the variations you put into it. Clean mechanics can't rescue a weak variation. Ground the variation in an observed problem, write a hypothesis that links cause to behaviour, match the claim to what changed, and add guardrail metrics so a 'win' on clicks doesn't hide a loss elsewhere.

    When the meeting stalls and someone says "let's just A/B test it", don't refuse. Slow the team down enough to ask what the variation is actually testing.

    Ground the variation

    Weak: "we think this version will perform better." Strong: "users miss the primary action because the label is vague and disconnected from the task; a specific label, with placement, size and colour unchanged, will move more users to the next step." Usability tests, interviews, analytics and session replay supply the observed problem. A research-informed variation that loses still teaches you something.

    Match the claim to the change

    • One element changed → claim about that element.
    • Full redesign → "this package beat that package", not "the label caused it".
    • Several unrelated changes, no hypothesis → you know who won, not why.
    • Need each variable's effect → multivariate test or staged plan.

    When several things change

    Calabro's 2024 tutorial argues many 'A/B' questions are really multivariate: define variables (layout, theme), their variants (grid/list, light/dark) and test every combination, so you can see each element's effect and how they interact — instead of changing one thing per test for months.

    Clicks are not successPair the primary metric with guardrails — task completion downstream, errors, support contacts, trust signals — or the test becomes a machine for optimising local behaviour at the expense of the experience.
    RememberA test only compares what you put in — base variations on an observed problem.

    A/B Test Sample Sizing

    Calculating sample size in the real world

    Summary There is no standard A/B sample size. It comes from the current baseline rate, the smallest improvement worth shipping (the minimum detectable effect), and the chosen confidence and power. The MDE is a product decision, so stakeholders belong in that conversation.

    Calabro's example: 18% of users complete the task today. Moving to 19% wouldn't justify a redesign; the team agrees that about 26% would. That 8-point improvement is the MDE, and it's a business judgement about what's worth the work, not a statistical setting.

    • Baseline from analytics if you have it; a reasoned estimate beats ignoring it.
    • MDE set with the product team: "how much better must B be for this to be worth shipping?"
    • Confidence and power follow your organisation's agreed standard.
    • Explain the final number to stakeholders in plain language — what it can and can't detect.

    Rule of thumb

    Halving the MDE roughly quadruples the sample. For the 18% → 26% example at 80% power, a standard two-proportion calculation gives roughly 330 users per variant at 90% confidence and about 420 at 95% (our calculation, not the article's) — which is why chasing 1-point lifts on low-traffic flows rarely makes sense.

    Calabro's earlier startup cheat sheet recommended much smaller samples for fast directional learning in MVPs. This guidance is for defensible tests in a live product.

    Statistically Significant or Not?

    Significance belongs to findings, not studies

    Summary A study isn't 'significant' as a whole. Each comparison in it is, or isn't, depending on effect size, variability, sample size and the confidence level chosen. One survey can produce three significant findings and seven that aren't — from the same participants.
    01
    Sample size

    How much information you have.

    02
    Effect size

    How big the observed difference is. Bigger is easier to separate from noise.

    03
    Variability

    How consistent responses are. Noisy data needs more people.

    04
    Confidence level

    The threshold the organisation agreed for calling a result real.

    Significance also doesn't tell you a result is large or important — the American Statistical Association warns against reading it that way. Report the observed difference, the uncertainty and the practical consequence together.

    Check Your Tool’s Math

    From “Microsoft Forms Did Me Dirty”

    Summary In a 32-person ranking survey, Microsoft Forms named a winner the raw data didn't support. Its undocumented positional weighting rewarded items that often landed near the top, not the item most people preferred. Different aggregation methods legitimately crown different winners, so researchers need to know which one fits the question.

    He exported responses, rebuilt the rankings and compared methods: pairwise wins with a Borda tie-breaker (chosen — easy to explain), and the Schulze method (handles preference cycles, harder to reproduce). LLMs were useful for surfacing candidate methods. Because the deadline forced a small sample, he'd agreed beforehand to present results as directional and avoid claims about small differences.

    Ranking, done right

    Calabro's earlier pieces on force ranking (2023) and fixing rank data (2024) make the same point: simple plurality only counts first choices and can crown an option most people dislike; averaging weighted ranks is sensitive to outliers. Pairwise methods — Schulze, ranked pairs — respect the majority's head-to-head preferences. In one B2C study, 100+ ranked responses in two days settled a long debate about what belongs on a product card.

    • Defaults are a choice someone else made. Know what the tool calculates before you report it.
    • Agreeing the evidence standard before data collection prevents fights at the readout.
    • Show the method and its limits — transparency is what makes the result credible.
    • As more analysis is automated, polished output can still answer the wrong question.

    5 Things AI Still Can’t Do for UX Research

    Why expertise still matters

    Summary AI can automate research activities; it can't yet produce trustworthy findings on its own. Calabro names five gaps: replacing real users, writing good research questions, modelling complex interactions, choosing the right statistics, and interpreting results against the decision.
    1. Replace real users
      Synthetic users sound human and confirm what you already believe. They can't get confused, invent a workaround or surprise you — where the best findings come from.
    2. Design good research questions

    3. Model complex interactions
      The GOMS-KLM experiment took about six times longer to correct than to do by hand. Newer models may do better.
    4. Choose the right statistics

    5. Interpret findings

    How Many Users Do I Need?

    Summary For qualitative work the honest answer is 'until new sessions stop changing what we know' — saturation.     Saturation

    Saturation is the point where new data stops changing what you know — not everyone saying the same thing, but the core themes holding steady. Signs: repetition; your framework stops changing; you ask "what surprised me in that last session?" and the answer is "not much" two or three times running; edge cases confirm themes instead of opening new questions.

    02
    Benchmark

    03

    5 out of 5 Isn't 100%

    Confidence intervals, error bars and realistic completion benchmarks

    Summary Five engineers completed the new export flow: '100% success'. The 95% confidence interval was 48–100%. Confidence intervals turn 'trust me' into a range, shift the conversation from 'is five significant?' to 'how much risk can we accept?', and error bars put that range on the chart.

    Task completion → Wilson score interval

    Completion is binary, so use the Wilson interval (it behaves at 0% and 100%, where simpler formulas break). Enter 1/0 per participant, take the average and count, pick z (1.645 for 90%, 1.96 for 95%) and apply the Wilson formula — Calabro shares a spreadsheet version. For time on task, use a log-normal approach because timing data is skewed (see Time-on-Task).

    Error bars

    Put the interval on the chart. A false-alert rate of 12% sounds minor; 9–15% across traders, with some well above average, changes the priority. Tight bars mean a predictable experience; wide bars mean some users are hit much harder — or that you need more data before comparing two designs.

    What's a good completion rate?

    Not Six Sigma — manufacturing's 3.4 defects per million doesn't map to human behaviour. Calabro's rule of thumb: about 90% for first-time users on core tasks, higher for experienced users on tasks they do daily. Below 50–60% for novices is a red flag. Track the change between versions rather than chasing perfection.

    Summary Surveys are fast, scalable and look great on a slide, which is why they get asked to do jobs they can't.     Correlation Is a Signal, Not a Cause

    How to use patterns in messy data without over-claiming

    Summary Users who finished the onboarding tutorial had much higher three-month retention, so the team credited the tutorial. Maybe — or motivated users both finish tutorials and stay.     Stats With LLMs: Expert in the Loop

    Which test answers which question — and what the AI can't decide for you

    Summary LLMs remove the software friction from statistics: upload a CSV, describe the comparison, and the model writes and runs code.     The workflow

    1. You describe the analysis
      Including the design: independent or paired, how many groups, what the outcome is.
    2. The LLM prepares data and writes code
      Ask it to show every change it made — it may silently delete an outlier or fill a missing value.
    3. Python runs the calculation
      Deterministic: same data, same code, same result.

    Pendo Without the Tagging Mess

    A rollout playbook: strategy, top tasks, funnels, then everything else

    Summary Pendo usually ends up as an in-app messaging tool or a pile of tags nobody trusts. Calabro's fix is to set the strategy before tagging anything: ask executives and product teams what they need to see, narrow the product to 10–15 top tasks, tag those by hand as features, build 3–6-step funnels, and give leaders a dashboard. Guides, Paths and Journeys come after the baseline.

    Why "tag everything" fails

    • Auto-tagging tracks layout, not intent. In large custom-built apps it produces vague, inconsistent names (div1, buttonX). They flow into funnels and dashboards and give the illusion of tracking. Calabro's advice: leave it out of the internal docs and assume all tagging is manual.
    • Every tag is an assumption. The same Submit button tagged three ways (submitButton_v1, btn-submit-primary, untagged) makes one product show a 20% drop-off and another none, because of set-up rather than UX.
    • A long list of actions can't be prioritised or tied to business goals, and it doesn't roll up into an executive view. Teams that each invent their own approach end up with fragmented data that can't scale.

    1 · Start with strategy, not tags

    01
    Ask executives

    What user behaviour do you need to see? Which metrics are already reported across business units, and which are missing?

    02
    Ask product teams

    Where do users get stuck? How do people move through complex workflows? Where does behaviour differ from what you expected?

    Write a shared tagging plan

    2 · Top task analysis

    1. List the common tasks
      Aim for the roughly 20% of actions that make up about 80% of use.
    2. Add rare but high-impact tasks
      Exporting an audit log is rare, but it matters for trust and compliance.
    3. Trim to 10–15 core tasks
      Merge similar tasks, keep the most complex or representative one, and favour tasks that take different paths through the product.

    01
    Funnels

    02

    Calculating the ROI of UX

    A 5-step framework template for showing how UX work saves money and drives revenue

    Summary Calculating ROI isn't inherently a UX function — but it's squarely within UX's strengths. Researchers who can translate behavior, outcomes, and decisions into financial impact will have far more organizational influence than those who only report insights.

    UX research doesn't get the recognition it deserves partly because most UX teams don't speak the language their organizations actually care about: money. ROI calculations connect better user experiences to stronger financial outcomes — and when done well, they make the case that UX isn't just about making things work better, it's about making money.

    The 5-Step Framework

    1. Identify the Problem and Establish Benchmarks What specific problem are you solving? Gather current-state data: time on task, error rates, support call volume, conversion rates. These become your before-measurement.
    2. Define Goals and Expected Outcomes Set measurable goals that reflect both user improvement and business impact. Almost every goal maps to either saving money or driving revenue. Example: reduce support calls by 15% (savings) or increase feature adoption by 20% (revenue).
    3. Gather Data to Estimate ROI Collect organizational data to quantify financial impact. Identify the cost of the problem: support hours × hourly cost, conversion loss × average order value, error rate × time-to-recover × number of affected users.
    4. Calculate the ROI ROI = (Benefits − Costs) / Costs × 100. Benefits = financial value of the improvement. Costs = research, design, and implementation investment. Document your assumptions — this transparency builds credibility.
    5. Report Findings Effectively Lead with the number. Follow with the evidence. End with the recommendation. Stakeholders don't need the methodology — they need the impact and the ask.

    The Most Common ROI Calculation Errors

    1) Attributing 100% of a business outcome to UX work. Always estimate a conservative contribution percentage. 2) Using inflated time estimates. Verify with actual data. 3) Not accounting for implementation costs. ROI is net of investment, not gross benefit.

    RememberTranslate the friction into money: cost × frequency × people affected.

    UX Research Debriefs Reframed

    Two different meetings: synthesis first, strategy second

    Summary Teams blur two kinds of debrief to save time, and get the worst of both.     A strategic debrief, in three lines

    • Finding: appraisers broke their workflow into three unofficial stages (Data, Narrative, Review).

    Reframing Dark Patterns: 2025 Edition

    UX's Real Horror Story — naming them isn't enough, we need a principle

    Summary The UX field has spent years fighting unethical design by naming it. "Roach Motel." "Confirmshaming." Those names helped at first, but naming bad practices hasn't stopped them — if anything, dark patterns multiplied. The fix is a principle-first approach grounded in user control.

    The UX field spent years fighting unethical design by naming it. "Roach Motel." "Confirmshaming." "Bait and Switch." The idea was that if we could expose these behaviors with catchy names, we could shame teams into stopping them. That strategy helped at first — shared vocabulary made it easier to point out predatory practices. But naming hasn't stopped them. If anything, dark patterns multiplied.     The real problem: these names describe surface behavior, not underlying intent. Once a design gets labeled a dark pattern, the discussion ends with moral outrage instead of action. The three questions: Can they stop? Can they undo? Can they refuse? If any answer is "no," the product is already in manipulative territory regardless of what the taxonomy calls it.

    02

    UX Is Thankless and That's a Good Thing

    Reframing using hedonic adaptation theory

    Summary UX work often feels thankless because people quickly adjust to improvements and forget what came before. Hedonic adaptation explains this pattern — and gives us a healthier way to understand our impact.

    UX work is thankless. Good UX is invisible to the end user. Both statements are true. But the idea that they're a bad thing is worth challenging.

    A few years ago, a team improved a painful five-minute workflow that users struggled with every day — flagged in interviews, surveys, support tickets, and usage analytics. The redesign dropped time-on-task to under two minutes, error rates fell, and there was immediate positive user feedback. The team felt proud. Some teammates expected the positive sentiment to last — for that "big win" to shift how the org perceived UX moving forward. Within a week, people moved on. The new design became the assumed baseline expectation. From the user's perspective, it just worked correctly now. Not an improvement. Just fixing what was broken.

    What Is Hedonic Adaptation?

    Hedonic adaptation is the human tendency to return to a stable level of satisfaction after something in life changes. A positive change feels meaningful for a short period, then the feeling fades as the new experience becomes familiar. The mind recalibrates, sets a new baseline, and treats the improvement as the normal state.

    This is exactly what happens with UX work. When friction is removed, the reminder of UX effort goes with it. It's not a failure of communication or a lack of recognition — it's something deeper and more universal.

    The Reframe

    What hedonic adaptation doesn't mean is that the work had less value. It means the improvement blended into daily use — which is the goal. Success in UX most often looks like something that feels obvious after the fact. The measure isn't applause. It's behavioral change.

    UX feels thankless because the success of the work is often built on removing user friction. When the friction is gone, the reminder of UX effort goes with it. — Trevor Calabro

    The Metric Strategy

    Before any significant UX project, document the current-state metrics — not to establish blame, but to have a before-measurement. After the work ships, collect the same metrics. The delta is your visibility. This is why ROI frameworks matter even for researchers who "just want to do good work." Hedonic adaptation will erase the felt sense of improvement. The data preserves it.

    RememberGood UX gets forgotten because people adapt. That's the win.

    Weaponized Institutional Knowledge in UX

    A hot take rant

    Summary Institutional knowledge is often treated as a strength, but in practice it's one of the most common ways UX best practices get ignored, undermined, or shut down. This post breaks down how "but our users are different" becomes a weapon — and explains why it's time to shift the burden of proof back where it belongs.

    Institutional knowledge has become a sacred cow in many organizations. Praised. Protected. Framed as a strength. And on paper it makes sense — knowledge built up over time through real experience is valuable. But in the real world, institutional knowledge often gets weaponized, especially against UX researchers.

    It's used as a conversation ender, not a conversation starter. A justification instead of a hypothesis. A way to dismiss UX fundamentals instead of engaging with them.

    The 90/10 Rule of UX Best Practices

    UX best practices aren't arbitrary. They come from decades of research and behavioral studies conducted with real users in controlled environments. The Nielsen Norman Group conducted a macro analysis of their best practice recommendations and found that following their published foundational usability guidelines works out in the real world roughly 90% of the time.

    That leaves about 10% of cases where breaking a best practice might result in a better user experience. Not will. Not likely to. Might. So why do so many product teams ignore best practices by default? Why are these patterns still common in 2025?

    • Icon-only navigation with no labels
    • Hamburger menus in large desktop viewports
    • Critical controls that only appear on hover
    • Unprompted UI animations
    • Forms that don't explain errors
    • Patterns proven to fail 30 years ago, still in use today

    The answer, in most cases, is weaponized institutional knowledge.

    How It Works

    Early in Trevor's career, comments like "our users are different" felt like an opening — maybe someone had seen something interesting, had data, had an insight. Leaning in with "I'd love to learn more — how did we figure that out?" was met not with excitement but friction. Not disagreement. Not skepticism. Territorialism. A sense that a design decision had been challenged — and with it, someone's rank in the organization.

    "I've been here for ten years" is not data. It's a power move.

    That's why I call it weaponized. Because it's used to stop research. To block validation. To override evidence. In many cases, it's a shortcut to winning an argument with no data at all. — Trevor Calabro

    How to Counter It

    Ask for the original research. "What did users show us when we tried this?" is a different question than "did users like it?" If there was no research, the objection is opinion dressed as fact. If there was research, demand the context — user populations, business constraints, and technology all change. Past failure under different conditions is not evidence of future failure under current conditions. The burden of proof belongs with the person claiming their users are different. Make them provide it.

    Remember'Our users are different' needs evidence; best practice is the default.

    UX Research as a Service

    “Hear me out, please”

    Summary Service doesn't mean taking orders. It means judgment, standards and responsibility for better outcomes — showing up while projects are shaped, pairing through design, sorting uncertainty early, tying findings to project choices and checking after launch.     Shaping — six questions

    • What problem are we actually solving, and who is affected?
    • Which decisions are already made, and where can direction still change?
    • What evidence already exists — past studies, analytics, tickets, sales notes?
    • Which assumptions survive mainly to keep momentum?

    Ideas become workflows, goals become screens, loose requirements become decisions about hierarchy and content — that's where wrong assumptions surface.     Confirm or Undo?

    Guardrails that don't slow everyone down

    Summary Choose by frequency and severity. Frequent, low-impact actions get Undo; rare, severe ones get a confirmation — typed if the stakes are high. Label the buttons with the outcome, not 'Yes' or 'Confirm', and never hide Undo in a toast that fades away.
    • Only a few actions are truly irreversible: deleting linked items, removing data from other accounts, disconnecting devices, leaving without saving.
    • If you can't make it undoable, delay it — e.g. 15 minutes before a permanent delete runs.
    • Specific verbs: Delete, Leave, Unsubscribe, Stay. Add "forever" or "permanently" when it is.
    • No double negatives ("Cancel cancellation?").
    • For high-severity actions, ask users to type a name to break the instinctive click.
    • Undo as a persistent button, not only a disappearing toast.

    Choosing the Selection Control

    Extends “UX Has a Basics Problem”

    Summary Radio for a question with one answer, checkbox for a statement or independent options, toggle only when the change applies immediately. Never pre-select. Dropdowns for long single-choice lists, segmented controls for two to five short options.
    01
    Radio

    Label is a question; exactly one answer; options equally weighted; good for required input.

    02
    Checkbox

    Label is a statement; multi-select or a single confirmation; good for optional input.

    03
    Toggle

    Binary setting that takes effect immediately.

    04
    Segmented

    2–5 short options, single select.

    05
    Dropdown

    Single choice from a long list (5+).

    06
    Chips

    Short multi-select, often filters.

    • Offer a way out: "None of the above", "I don't remember".

    Tables and Bulk Operations

    The B2B work most products get wrong

    Summary Complex tables need filtering, sorting, column control and density modes, with row actions as visible buttons rather than hover reveals. Bulk import succeeds or fails in its repair step: let users fix mismatches inside the flow instead of rejecting the file as 'invalid'.

    Tables

    • Start from tasks: which filters, sorts, search, editing modes and permissions are needed.
    • Let users build their own filter set; consider per-column filters for speed.
    • Test with very long and very short values and pseudo-localisation; offer compact/comfortable density.
    • Let users show, hide and reorder columns when comparison matters.
    • Avoid hover-only row actions — they cause errors and rage clicks. Use visible buttons on each row.
    • Check how screen readers move through the table.

    Bulk import

    • Define the attributes to map and, for each, a data profile and whether it's required.
    • Support Excel, CSV, copy-paste and preview; write specific errors for broken files and unmatched columns.
    • Before overwriting, flag duplicates and ask what to do.
    • Imports are hard to reverse, so tag imported records (source, date) so they can be found and fixed later.

    Calabro link

    Row hover actions fail the same test as hover menus in the Primary Navigation Guide; filtering and sorting being core rather than optional is misunderstanding #6 in Top 10 UX Misunderstandings.

    RememberB2B tables: visible actions, column control, density modes.

    Research Without User Access

    What you can still do in B2B and enterprise

    Summary When NDAs, procurement and security clearance block interviews and testing, there is still evidence: task analysis, existing workflows, job postings, shadowing similar roles, customer success and domain experts. And remember the buyer is rarely the user — there are two experiences to design.
    • Map workflows and pain points into a service blueprint.
    • Use search trends and public forums to find frequent problems.
    • Build an internal test lab with colleagues closest to customers.
    • Design for two audiences: the customer organisation buying and the employees using it.

    Start with trust

    Enterprise stakeholders are protective of data, IP and politics. Small, low-risk first steps build the trust that later opens access to users and logs. Pair with Calabro's minimum rigor threshold: be explicit about what proxy evidence can and can't support.

    Measuring Feature Impact (TARS)

    Target audience, Adoption, Retention, Satisfaction

    Summary Measure a feature against the users who have the problem it solves, not all users. Then how many of them meaningfully adopt it, how many keep using it, and how easy it felt (CES, asked only of retained users). TARS deliberately leaves out conversion — too many non-design factors move it.
    • Target: estimate from similar features — usage of the feature itself isn't the audience.
    • Adoption: count value signals (exports shared, filters used), not clicks or session time. A niche feature used by 50–75% of its 10% audience can be a success.
    • Retention: above 50% high strategic importance; 25–35% medium; 10–20% low.
    • Satisfaction: customer effort score, from "much harder" to "much easier than expected".

    UX targets to sit beside it

    Top-task success > 80%; time to first success < 90 s for onboarding; default settings corrected by < 10% of users; SUS > 75. Each team picks 3–4 KPIs that reflect its own work. Pairs with Calculating the ROI of UX and SUPR-Q.

    RememberTarget → Adoption → Retention → Satisfaction.

    AI Labelling Is Now Law

    EU AI Act transparency rules, in force since 2 August 2026

    Summary Any product whose AI output reaches people in the EU must tell users they're talking to a chatbot or agent, and label deepfakes and unreviewed AI-written text on matters of public interest. Most AI-assisted work is exempt. A sparkle icon is not a label: use the EU mark plus plain text, visible, persistent and accessible.
    • A skim before publishing isn't review; editing must be substantive and someone named takes responsibility.
    • Summaries, composite images, substantive rewrites and adding or removing elements in photos all count as generation.
    • Non-compliant: a faint icon, a footer note, a label that flashes and disappears. The disclosure must survive sharing and download.
    • The EU icon set has three variants: AI, fully AI-generated, partially AI-modified. Carbon's AI label (inline, icon, explainability panel) is a production-ready reference.

    Charts and Dashboards That Lead to Decisions

    Extends “Why Your Data Dashboard Stinks” and “Choosing the Right Chart Type”

    Summary Start from the decision. Pick the chart by purpose, then strip it to the data: direct labels, about five gridlines, bars from zero, one highlight colour on grey. Dashboards trade screen space against abstraction, pages and interaction — and sometimes the answer is an insight, not a dashboard.

    Chart design

    • Label data directly instead of using a legend; titles state the takeaway ("Churn rose 3 pts after the price change").
    • Bars start at zero; lines may not, if the range is obvious.
    • Grey for context, one colour for the point; ≤ 5 categorical colours; never rainbow scales; red/green only with a second cue.
    • Small multiples with shared scales instead of one crowded chart.

    Accurate

    03

    Accessible charts

    Contrast to WCAG; never colour alone; nothing available only on hover; a data table alternative; title, takeaway and units in text. Chartability's audit flags 14 critical failures out of 50 heuristics.

    Deciding How Much the AI Should Do

    Autonomy levels, trust scaffolding and the anatomy of a chat turn

    Summary Autonomy is a product decision. Grant the lowest level that delivers the outcome — suggest, draft, confirm, act within limits, or run while monitored — judged by impact, reversibility, reliability, sensitivity and whether anyone would notice a bad outcome. Then scaffold trust for the moment people rely on the output.

    Trust is calibrated, not maximised

    01
    Truthful expectations

    Say what the AI does and doesn't do, in task terms.

    02
    Reasons & provenance

    Claim-level citations; why this recommendation.

    03
    Uncertainty & limits

    Plain-language ranges, not an unexplained 78%.

    04
    Safeguards & control

    Inspect → adjust → correct → reject → override → escalate → revoke.

    05
    Track record & repair

    History, outage disclosure, receipts for corrections.

    Is this chat turn complete?

    Context visible and editable · first-timers know what to ask · natural expression without prompt engineering · waiting, streaming and stop are clear · evidence is checkable · one part can be fixed without restarting · structure (table, checklist) when the job needs it · a clear path when the answer is wrong.
